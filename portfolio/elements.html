03 / THE BUILDING BLOCKS

The elements
of design.

Elements are the visual ingredients. Principles describe how we arrange them.

Line

A line creates a path, boundary or direction. Its thickness and style can communicate energy or calm.

Shape

A shape is a two-dimensional enclosed area. Geometric shapes are regular; organic shapes tend to be irregular.

Texture

Texture suggests how a surface looks or feels. Repeated marks can create a rough visual texture on a flat screen.

Space

Space includes the occupied area and the empty area around it. Negative space can improve clarity and draw attention to a focal point.

Form

Form has or suggests three dimensions: height, width and depth. Light and shading can make a flat drawing appear solid.

Value

Value describes how light or dark a colour is. Value differences can establish depth, emphasis and readable contrast.

Colour and typography as visual ingredients

Colour can group information, suggest a mood or draw attention, but its meaning depends on context and audience. A colour decision should also consider contrast and reproduction on screen or paper.

Typography is the selection and arrangement of type. Typeface, weight, size, line length, line spacing and letter spacing affect both expression and readability. Although typography is sometimes treated separately from the basic visual elements, it is central to graphics design.

Understanding the distinctions

DistinctionExplanation
Shape versus formA square is a flat shape; a shaded cube suggests three-dimensional form. A screen illustration can suggest depth without physically having it.
Texture versus patternTexture suggests a surface quality. A pattern is a repeated arrangement; repeating marks can create a visual texture.
Positive versus negative spacePositive space contains the subject or visual material. Negative space surrounds or separates it and can itself form a meaningful shape.
Hue versus valueHue identifies a colour family; value describes lightness or darkness. Different hues can have similar values and therefore weak light–dark contrast.
Line weight versus line directionWeight affects visual strength; direction can suggest stability, movement or tension. These are related but different properties.

Applying elements to the required artifacts

  • House with Lines: use horizontal and vertical lines for structure, diagonal lines for the roof, and deliberate line weights for consistency.
  • Basket of Fruits: combine organic shapes, overlapping space and changes in value to suggest volume.
  • Girl and House: use scale, form and foreground/background placement to create a readable scene.
  • Cartoon Graphic: simplify shapes and use expressive line direction to communicate character.
  • Working with Text: control typography, spacing and alignment so the intended reading order is clear.

Typography checklist

DecisionReason
Choose a clear hierarchyDistinguish title, section headings and body text without making every line equally prominent.
Limit unnecessary variationToo many typefaces and decorative effects can undermine consistency.
Use readable body textTest actual viewing size, line spacing and background contrast rather than choosing by appearance alone.
Align deliberatelyA shared edge makes related text easier to scan. Avoid accidental changes between alignments.
Avoid stretching letterformsUse proper type weights and sizes rather than distorting text horizontally or vertically.
Keep important text as text where practicalSelectable text is generally easier to search, resize and access than text baked into an image.
